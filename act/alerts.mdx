---
title: "Working alerts"
description: "Assign, move, comment on and close alerts from your own case system."
---

```
POST /v1/governance/alerts/{id}/{action}
```

Scope: `alerts:write`.

| Action | Body | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `assign` | `{"assignee": "<Orchid user id>" \| null, "note"?}` | to someone in your firm, or to no one. A new alert moves to `in_review`. |
| `status` | `{"status": "new" \| "in_review" \| "escalated", "note"?}` | reopening a closed alert needs a `note` saying why |
| `comment` | `{"note": "..."}` | |
| `close` | `{"disposition": "no_issue" \| "false_positive" \| "breach", "note"?, "rules"?}` | a breach needs a `note`. `rules` gives a different reason for one of the alert's rules: `{"<rule id>": "false_positive"}` |

The answer is `{"alert": {...}}`, the alert as it now stands.

## What a key cannot do

- **Sign off.** If your firm asks for a second reviewer on escalated alerts and breaches, a close by a key waits for a person's sign-off in the console, as any proposed close does.
- **Teach the rules.** When a person closes an alert, their judgement feeds the weekly rule proposals. A close by a key does not.

## On the record

Every change is an `alert.changed` event, with `attrs.via` naming the key, so the alert's history shows that your case system made it.

```bash
curl -X POST https://app.orchid.ac/v1/governance/alerts/$ALERT_ID/comment \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $ORCHID_KEY" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"note": "Case CMP-2291 opened in ServiceNow"}'
```
